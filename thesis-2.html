From Activity to Evidence

Why organisations that build, ship and roll things out cannot tell whether anything changed, what impact actually means as a quantity, and what has to exist before the question can be answered at all.

Section 1

The thesis

Organisations are not short of data. They are short of evidence that anything they did made a difference, and they are short of it at the exact moment somebody asks.

The infrastructure problem is solved. Ingestion, warehousing, orchestration, modelling and presentation are mature, competitive and getting cheaper every year. An organisation of almost any size can now collect more information about what people do than anyone in it can hold in mind.

What has not improved at the same rate is the ability to answer one question: did any of it change what people do? That question is not technical. It is a question about measurement design, about causal reasoning, and about human behaviour, and those are different disciplines from the ones that built the pipeline.

So the gap widens. More is collected, less is concluded, and the moment of reckoning arrives on a schedule nobody controls: a renewal, a budget round, a board meeting, a funder's report.

Core belief. Evidence is only valuable if it holds up in a room you are not in. Everything else is reassurance.
Section 2

Delivered is not the same as changed

The same failure appears in three costumes. In each case the thing being counted is the effort rather than the effect.

They builtThey measureThey cannot answer
A product or featureUsage, adoption, funnel metricsWhether behaviour changed, or a metric moved
A platform or systemLicences, logins, dashboards builtWhether anyone works or decides differently
A programme or serviceAttendance, completion, satisfactionWhether anything is different afterwards

Satisfaction data deserves particular attention, because it is the most widely collected and the most routinely mistaken for evidence. It is gathered in the room, at the end, by the people who delivered the thing, from participants who know what answer is expected. It is cheap, immediate and almost always positive. It tells you the session landed. It cannot tell you anything happened afterwards.

Feedback is produced by people with an interest in the answer. That does not make it dishonest. It makes it inadmissible.

Underneath all three sits a structural problem rather than a lazy one. Measurement is almost always specified after the thing has been designed, and frequently after it has already run. By then the starting point no longer exists to be measured, and the only honest answer to "did it work" is that nobody can tell.

Section 3

Impact, as a quantity

Impact is not a feeling about whether something went well. It is a quantity, and writing it down makes the missing parts obvious.

b₂ − b₁

The measured difference on the behaviour that matters, and the only component that cannot be recovered after the fact.

r · repetition

How often it recurs, across how many people, and for how long it holds. A change that happens once is an anecdote.

e · effort

What it took to produce. The denominator is what turns a result into a decision about whether to do it again.

Section 4

The three components

post minus pre, and the problem of attribution

A single reading describes a state. It cannot demonstrate change, because change is a comparison and a comparison needs two points. This is why a missing baseline is the most expensive gap in measurement: every other failure can be corrected later, and this one cannot.

But a before and an after are not sufficient either. Organisations are not laboratories. While a programme runs, restructures happen, managers change, seasons turn and markets move. A metric that rose after an intervention may have been rising already.

Attribution does not require eliminating those explanations, which is rarely possible outside a trial. It requires naming them and showing why they do not account for what was observed. A delayed-start group, a non-participating team, or a pre-existing trend line is usually enough to make the argument stand.

repetition, and why one result is not a finding

A change observed once in one group is an anecdote with a number attached. Impact accumulates through recurrence: how many people, how often, and whether it survives beyond the period when everyone was paying attention.

This is where most claims quietly fail. Effects that look strong at week two frequently disappear by week eight, because what was measured was novelty rather than behaviour change. Any serious measure has to be collected more than once, and the interval matters as much as the instrument.

effort, and the denominator nobody reports

Almost every impact claim stops at the numerator. But a modest change produced cheaply can be worth considerably more than a large one that consumed a year, and a decision about whether to continue cannot be made without both halves.

Including cost also does something useful to the conversation. It moves the question from "did this work" to "was this worth doing, compared with what else we could have done," which is the question the person holding the budget was asking all along.

Section 5

The rule that holds both halves together

Evaluation without data is gut feeling with a chart attached. Data without evaluation is numbers that drive nothing.

These two failures look like opposites and are usually found in the same building.

The first belongs to people who understand their domain deeply and have never been asked to prove it. They know their programme works, and they are often right, but their evidence is testimony. It survives a friendly reader and collapses under a sceptical one.

The second belongs to organisations that have invested heavily in infrastructure and report diligently every month. The numbers are accurate, well presented and change nobody's mind, because no one ever specified what question they were meant to answer or what decision would follow from the answer.

Both are solvable, and the solution is the same: decide what should change, decide how you would know, and record the starting point before anything happens.

Section 6

What can be evaluated

Three levels. The method is identical at each; the unit of analysis and the people involved are what change.

Level 01

A product or feature

Something shipped. Did it change what users do, or did a metric move while behaviour stayed where it was?

Level 02

A project or programme

Something delivered to people over time. Training, onboarding, a service change, a funded intervention. Did it hold, and for whom?

Level 03

A team

How a team decides, challenges and acts on what it knows. The most under-measured thing in any data-rich organisation.

The third level is the one organisations arrive at last and need most. An organisation can have excellent instrumentation, accurate reporting and a capable analytics team, and still change nothing, because findings lose to seniority, arrive without narrative, or land on someone with no authority to act. That is a behavioural problem with a substantial research literature behind it and almost no commercial practice.

Section 7

How the work runs

Three engagements. Not a sequence everyone travels; most organisations start at whichever point matches where they already are.

01 · Diagnose

Find out what is true

Something has already run. We examine one initiative and establish what can honestly be claimed about it, what cannot, and where the measurement breaks. No access to client data is required, which means nothing waits on a security review.

A written assessment, a recorded walkthrough that can be forwarded, and one working session.
02 · Design

Build what is missing

Nothing has been designed yet, which is the best possible moment. We define the outcome, build the logic model, design and test the measures, and specify the baseline to be recorded before delivery starts. Measurement built in advance is worth several times measurement bolted on afterwards.

Outcome definition, up to three tested measures, baseline specification, and reporting built for the person who will not be in the room.
03 · Track

Keep it running

The definitions exist and now have to keep answering, on the cycle the organisation actually runs on rather than one we invent. This is where measurement stops being a project and becomes infrastructure.

Collection running across existing systems, readings on your renewal or board cycle, and reporting others can read without us.
Section 8

Where it ends up

The end state is not a report somebody waits for. It is a system the organisation owns and its own analyst runs.

01

Ingest

Sources connected and flowing automatically. No manual exports, no monthly scramble.

02

Store

A warehouse holding the history, so that a before and an after both exist by default.

03

Model

Preprocessing and the behavioural definitions that make the numbers mean something specific.

04

Ask

A portal or agent the team questions directly, without waiting on an analyst to produce a chart.

None of that infrastructure is ours, and that is deliberate. Ingestion, warehousing and presentation are solved problems served by excellent vendors, and inventing a worse version would be a poor use of everyone's money.

What we contribute is the layer across the top: what gets measured, how it is defined, how behavioural signals combine, what gets surfaced and what decision it is meant to trigger. The components underneath are replaceable. That layer is not.

We set it up alongside your own analyst rather than becoming one. A system that depends on us is a system we have failed to hand over.

Section 9

What this is not

It is not a research project. The standard is commercial defensibility rather than academic certainty, which is a lower bar than publication and a far higher one than satisfaction scores. The test is whether a sceptical finance lead, reading alone and looking for a reason to cut, would come away persuaded.

It is not a rebuild of your stack. Your engineers are better at that, and if the infrastructure genuinely does not work, that is the first problem and we will say so rather than measure on top of it.

It is not guaranteed to tell you what you hoped. Measurement designed properly can show that something championed internally changed nothing. The value is finding out early enough to redesign it, and an engagement where that outcome would be unwelcome is one we should not start.

On evidence of our own. Previous client results sit under confidentiality, and publishing figures that cannot be attributed would contradict the argument this document makes. Design-partner work with publication rights agreed at the outset is under way, and results will appear when they are real, attributable and permitted. A document arguing for defensible evidence should be held to its own standard.
